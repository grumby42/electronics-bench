Lineage: from the first battery to today

Build the oldest versions of the ideas behind modern electronics, then work forward. Each build grew out of the one before it. Every one is wired from kit parts and runs on the real circuit solver, so you can break it and see why it fails.

How did they find their moments — and did they know?

How the moments came. Almost never from nowhere. They came from a new instrument that showed something no one had seen (Ørsted’s needle), an argument someone wanted to win (Volta), a need someone would pay for (the telegraph, the telephone network), or years of preparation that made a small surprise meaningful (Faraday, Bardeen and Brattain). The Learn app’s “Lost, kept & eureka” lesson tells those stories.

Did they know? Three patterns, marked on every build:

Knew at once — Volta, Ørsted and Morse each saw immediately that they had something important, though none foresaw the modern world.

Saw part of it — Fleming, the relay builders and Bell Labs knew what their invention did, but not what it would become.

Recognised later — Boole died in 1864; his algebra ran circuits only from 1937. Maxwell died in 1879; Hertz proved his radio waves in 1887. Charles Sanders Peirce saw around 1880 that one kind of gate could build all the others, but he never published it, and Henry Sheffer published the idea independently in 1913. Being right and being recognised are often decades apart.

How it works

This recreates the kit's function, not a photo. The panel exposes the same connection points as the real lab: six stepped battery taps (V1=1.5 V … V6=9 V), the negative rail, the breadboard (rows A–V, columns 1–30, every block of five holes is one node), and the fixed modules with their numbered spring terminals.

Live nodes. Hover or tap-hold any point to see every point it is electrically tied to glow amber — that is the union-find node engine the kit teaches you to reason about.

Simulation. Switch on power and the analog solver (nodal analysis with diode/LED forward-drop and transistor-as-switch models) computes real node voltages every change, so LED brightness, the meter and tone respond to what you actually wired. Capacitor-timed circuits (multivibrator, oscillators) use behavioral timing models.

Two layouts. Desktop puts the panel beside the bins; portrait phones stack the panel over a bottom tab bar and scale the SVG to fit. Pinch-zoom is enabled.
